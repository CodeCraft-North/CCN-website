The honest answer to “how much does a small-business website cost?” is that the price depends on the job the website needs to do.

A focused five-page site for a referral-led consultant is a different project from a lead-generation website with detailed services, location content, integrations and an ongoing search strategy. Comparing them only by page count is like comparing two premises by the number of rooms.

The useful question is not simply how cheaply a website can be built. It is what you need to invest to create a credible website that performs its intended role and remains manageable afterwards.


## The short answer

Small-business website costs normally fall into two models:

- **One-off build:** a larger project payment, followed by separate hosting, maintenance and future-change costs
- **Website subscription:** a lower initial cost with hosting, support and an agreed level of ongoing work included in a monthly fee

## Price and cost are not quite the same

The price is the figure on the proposal. The cost also includes the time, risk and additional work needed to make the website useful.

A low-price build can become expensive if:

- The business has to rewrite every page afterwards
- Important features were not included
- The site is difficult to update or move
- Hosting and support were never discussed
- Poor performance or accessibility needs correcting
- The website attracts the wrong enquiries

The reverse is also true. A larger website is not automatically better value. Paying for complex animations, numerous pages or custom features only makes sense when they support a real business need.


## The main factors that affect website cost

### 1. The website's job

A website that mainly reassures referred prospects can be relatively focused. A site expected to generate demand through search needs more research, content and structure.

Start by choosing the [right type of small-business website](/resources/choosing-the-right-small-business-website/). The intended outcome affects almost every later decision.

### 2. Page count and page complexity

Five genuinely distinct pages usually require less work than fifteen. However, a complex pricing page or service comparison can take more thought than several straightforward information pages.

Ask whether “five pages” means five carefully planned pages or a template filled with text. The number alone does not describe the quality of the work.

### 3. Content responsibilities

Who decides what each page should say? Who supplies the first draft, images, testimonials and project details?

Content commonly delays website projects because responsibility was never made explicit. A quote should distinguish between:

- Content supplied ready to publish
- Collaborative structure and editing
- Full copywriting
- Ongoing article writing
- Formatting and publishing client-supplied material

Code Craft North's Growth plan includes help structuring, optimising and publishing articles supplied by the client. It does not include writing them from scratch.

### 4. Design and brand work

A website can use an established visual identity or help shape an incomplete one. Logo design, illustration, photography and a full branding project are separate disciplines and may need separate budgets.

Custom website design also involves more than choosing colours. Layout, hierarchy, mobile behaviour, reusable components and accessibility all take time.

### 5. Functionality and integrations

Contact forms are usually straightforward. Booking systems, payments, memberships, calculators, portals, multilingual content and connections to other business systems add complexity.

Third-party tools may also have their own monthly or transaction fees. The proposal should state whether those costs are included.

### 6. Search requirements

Basic on-page SEO should be part of a competent build: descriptive titles, logical headings, crawlable pages, useful internal links and good performance.

Broader search work is different. Keyword research, multiple service or location pages, content planning and authority building require continued effort. A website cannot include unlimited future SEO simply because the initial pages are “SEO friendly”.

The guide to [SEO basics for small businesses](/resources/seo-basics-for-small-businesses/) explains where those foundations end and ongoing work begins.

### 7. Hosting, maintenance and support

Every website needs someone responsible for:

- Hosting and renewals
- Backups and monitoring
- Security and technical updates
- Domain and DNS access
- Content changes
- Help when something breaks

A one-off quote may exclude all of those after launch. A managed plan may include most of them. Compare the responsibility, not only the first invoice.


## What a website quote should include

A clear proposal should tell you:

1. Which pages are included
2. What each side supplies
3. How many review stages are allowed
4. Which features and integrations are included
5. Who handles hosting and the domain
6. What happens after launch
7. How additional pages or changes are charged
8. When ownership transfers
9. What files and assets you receive
10. The expected delivery range and what can affect it

If a quote says only “professional five-page website”, too much remains open to interpretation.


## Costs that are easy to overlook

Depending on the project, allow for:

- Domain registration and renewal
- Business email
- Premium fonts, photography or illustration
- Booking, email-marketing or CRM services
- Payment-provider fees
- Copywriting
- Accessibility or legal advice
- Ongoing content production
- Future pages and functionality

Not every site needs all of these. The point is to identify them before comparing proposals.


## When a lower-cost option is sensible

A smaller budget is not a problem when the scope is honest.

A focused website can work well when:

- Most customers arrive through referrals
- The service range is small
- The content is ready
- No custom functionality is needed
- The primary goal is credibility and contact

Problems begin when a basic brochure budget is expected to fund a complex lead-generation strategy, ecommerce operation or bespoke application.

Choose a smaller website deliberately, not accidentally.


## Subscription or one-off: which offers better value?

A subscription can offer better value when the business wants continued support, regular existing-page changes and no technical responsibility.

A one-off build can offer better value when the content is stable, the upfront budget is available and the business is comfortable arranging hosting and future work.

The full [website subscription versus one-off comparison](/resources/website-subscription-vs-one-off-build-which-is-right-for-you/) covers ownership, exit terms and ongoing responsibility in more detail.


## How to set a sensible website budget

Work backwards from the outcome:

1. What should the website improve?
2. Which customers and services matter most?
3. What content and proof already exist?
4. Which functionality is genuinely necessary?
5. Who will look after the site after launch?
6. What would make the investment worthwhile?

Then ask providers to solve the same defined problem. Quotes are easier to compare when they are responding to the same outcome and responsibilities.


## The honest view

Do not choose a website solely because it has the lowest starting price. Do not choose the largest proposal because it sounds more comprehensive.

Choose the clearest scope that can achieve the job, from a provider who is specific about what happens before, during and after launch.
