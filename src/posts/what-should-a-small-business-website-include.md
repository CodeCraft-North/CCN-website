A small-business website does not need every fashionable feature. It needs enough clear information and proof for the right visitor to understand the offer, trust the business and take a sensible next step.

That sounds obvious, but many websites miss one of those three jobs. They describe the company without explaining the value, list services without evidence or look polished without making contact easy.


## The short answer

Most service-business websites should include:

- A clear homepage
- Focused service information
- An honest About page
- Evidence and trust signals
- A straightforward contact route
- Privacy and cookie information where required
- Fast, accessible and mobile-friendly delivery
- A clear next step on every important page

The exact number of pages depends on the business. The important point is that each page has a distinct purpose.


## 1. A homepage that explains the business quickly

The homepage should help a new visitor answer four questions:

1. What does this business do?
2. Who is it for?
3. Why should I trust it?
4. What should I do next?

Avoid opening with a slogan that could describe almost any company. “Solutions for your future” may sound polished, but it makes the visitor work too hard.

A stronger opening is specific about the service and outcome. Supporting information can then explain the approach, show evidence and guide different visitors towards the right page.

The homepage does not need to contain every detail. Its job is to orientate people and help them move confidently.


## 2. Service pages with enough detail to decide

If a service matters commercially, give it enough space.

A useful service page normally covers:

- The problem or need
- Who the service is suitable for
- What is included
- How the process works
- Important limitations or responsibilities
- Relevant proof
- Pricing or a clear explanation of how pricing is decided
- The next step

Putting every service into three sentences on one page may keep the site small, but it often leaves customers and search engines without enough context.

Separate pages are useful when services solve different problems or attract different searches. Do not create separate pages only to repeat the same copy under slightly different headings.


## 3. An About page that earns trust

People visit About pages when they are deciding whether the business feels credible and compatible.

Useful information can include:

- Who they will work with
- Relevant experience
- Why the business operates as it does
- Location and service area
- Qualifications, memberships or professional background
- A straightforward photograph
- What clients can expect from the working relationship

Small businesses often have an advantage here: they can be specific and human.

## 4. Evidence behind the claims

Statements such as “trusted”, “leading” or “results-driven” are weak without evidence.

Depending on the business, useful proof might include:

- Named projects
- Before-and-after examples
- Measurable outcomes with context
- Testimonials
- Reviews on independent platforms
- Relevant qualifications or accreditations
- Clients, partners or sectors served
- A clear explanation of the process

Use only proof you can substantiate. A smaller number of specific examples is more convincing than a wall of vague praise.

If confidentiality limits what you can show, explain the type of work, challenge and outcome without revealing sensitive information.


## 5. A contact route that fits the decision

The right call to action depends on how customers buy.

Possible next steps include:

- Send an enquiry
- Request a review
- Book an initial call
- Ask for a quote
- Visit the premises
- Buy or book online

Do not ask for more information than you need for the first response. Long forms can be appropriate for complex qualification, but every field adds effort.

A good contact page should also set expectations:

- Who will reply
- How quickly
- What happens next
- Whether there is any obligation

The [Code Craft North contact page](/contact/) asks for enough context to give a useful reply while making optional details genuinely optional.


## 6. Clear business and contact details

Visitors should be able to confirm who they are dealing with.

Depending on the organisation, include:

- Trading or legal name
- Email address
- Phone number where appropriate
- Service area or business location
- Registered-company details where required
- Opening or response hours if they matter

Keep important information accurate wherever the business appears online. This helps customers and supports local search consistency.


## 7. Privacy, cookies and appropriate legal information

A contact form normally collects personal information such as a name and email address. The organisation should explain how that information is used.

The Information Commissioner's Office says small organisations that hold personal data generally need a privacy notice. It also explains that visitors must be told about cookies and that non-essential cookies normally need agreement before use. Its guidance on [privacy notices and cookies](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/advice-for-small-organisations/privacy-notices-and-cookies/cookies-and-privacy-notices-in-detail/) is a useful official starting point.

The exact pages depend on what the website does. An ecommerce site, membership platform or regulated service may have additional obligations. Government guidance sets out specific information businesses must provide when [selling online](https://www.gov.uk/online-and-distance-selling-for-businesses/online-selling).

Website developers can implement agreed notices and consent controls, but should not pretend generic template wording is tailored legal advice.

## 8. Mobile-friendly, accessible foundations

Mobile design is not the desktop page squeezed into a narrower column.

Check:

- Text remains comfortably readable
- Buttons and links are easy to tap
- Navigation works without precision
- Forms have clear labels and useful error messages
- Important content is not hidden
- Images have appropriate alternative text
- Keyboard users can reach interactive controls
- Colour contrast remains strong
- Layout does not overflow or move unexpectedly

Accessibility helps more people use the website and usually improves the experience for everyone. The requirements should be considered while planning and building, not added as a final plugin.


## 9. Good performance and dependable hosting

A useful website should load its meaningful content quickly and behave predictably.

That includes:

- Properly sized and compressed images
- Limited third-party scripts
- Stable layouts
- Sensible font loading
- Secure HTTPS delivery
- Backups and monitoring
- A named person responsible for technical issues

The guide to [why website speed matters](/resources/why-website-speed-matters-for-small-businesses/) explains how to prioritise performance work without treating a perfect score as the whole objective.


## 10. Search-ready structure

SEO foundations should be part of the site rather than a mysterious extra added after launch.

Each important page needs:

- A distinct purpose
- A concise, descriptive title
- One clear main heading
- Logical subheadings
- Useful links to related pages
- Crawlable content
- A unique description
- A clean, stable URL

The copy should use the language customers understand, but it should not repeat keywords until it sounds unnatural.

Local businesses may also benefit from genuinely useful location content and an accurate Google Business Profile. The [small-business SEO guide](/resources/seo-basics-for-small-businesses/) covers those areas in more detail.


## What a small-business website does not automatically need

Do not add something only because competitors have it.

Many businesses do not need:

- A homepage carousel
- Live chat
- An account area
- Dozens of location pages
- A blog with no publishing plan
- Complex animation
- Online booking
- A visual page builder
- Every social-media feed embedded on the site

Each feature should solve a customer or operational problem. Otherwise it adds distraction, maintenance and potential performance cost.


## A practical pre-launch checklist

Before the site goes live, check:

1. Can a new visitor explain the offer after reading the opening section?
2. Does every important service have enough information?
3. Are the claims supported by real evidence?
4. Is contact easy on mobile and desktop?
5. Do all forms work and explain what happens next?
6. Are business details accurate?
7. Are privacy and cookie requirements handled appropriately?
8. Do images, links and navigation work?
9. Does each page have one clear main heading and unique metadata?
10. Is responsibility for hosting, monitoring and future changes agreed?


## Keep the website proportionate

A strong small-business website is not defined by its number of pages. It is defined by how clearly it supports a real customer decision.

Start with the essential journey and information. Add pages or functionality when there is evidence they will be useful.
