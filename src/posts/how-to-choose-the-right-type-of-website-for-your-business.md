Choosing a website becomes much easier once you stop asking what it should look like and start asking what job it needs to do.

A local consultant who wins work through referrals does not need the same website as a business that depends on regular search enquiries. Neither needs the same setup as a retailer processing hundreds of online orders.

The right small-business website is the simplest one that can credibly achieve the outcome you need.


## The short answer

Most small-business websites fall into one of three broad types:

- **Brochure website:** builds trust and helps referred prospects check you out
- **Lead-generation website:** attracts, qualifies and converts potential customers
- **Ecommerce website:** allows people to choose, pay for and manage purchases online

There is overlap. A brochure website can generate enquiries and an ecommerce site still needs to build trust. The distinction is about the website's primary job, because that should shape its content, structure and budget.


## Start with the business outcome

Before discussing pages or features, answer this:

**What needs to be different because this website exists?**

A useful answer is specific enough to guide decisions. For example:

- Referred prospects should understand the service before calling
- More suitable local customers should find the business through search
- The team should spend less time answering the same early questions
- Customers should be able to buy without speaking to anyone

“We need to look more professional” is a reasonable starting point, but it is not quite enough. Professional to whom, and what should that person do next?

Once the outcome is clear, it becomes easier to decide which type of website is appropriate.

## When a brochure website is enough

A brochure website supports a reputation that is being built elsewhere. It reassures people who have already heard about the business through referrals, networking, tendering or offline activity.

It is often the right choice when:

- Most opportunities come through recommendation
- The offer is straightforward
- A conversation is needed before a customer can buy
- The website mainly needs to establish credibility
- Services and content change infrequently

A good brochure site is not an online leaflet. It still needs clear service information, useful proof, a strong mobile experience and an obvious next step. The difference is that it does not depend on a large content or search strategy to create demand.

## When you need a lead-generation website

A lead-generation website is expected to do more than reassure. It needs to help the right people discover the business, recognise that it fits their needs and take action.

That usually requires:

- Clear positioning for a defined audience
- Separate pages for important services or problems
- Evidence that supports the claims being made
- Calls to action that fit the buying decision
- Search-focused content where genuine demand exists
- Measurement so decisions are based on more than guesswork

The extra work is not simply “more pages”. It is the research and judgement needed to decide what those pages should say, how they connect and which visitors they are for.

## When ecommerce is the right choice

An ecommerce website is appropriate when the transaction itself should happen online.

It makes sense when customers need to:

- Browse a defined range of products
- Choose options or quantities
- Pay online
- Receive order confirmation or digital delivery
- Manage delivery, collection or account details

The visible shop is only part of the project. Product data, payments, tax, stock, fulfilment, returns, customer emails and ongoing administration all need thought.

That complexity is worthwhile when online purchasing genuinely improves the business model. It is usually unnecessary for bespoke, high-value services that need discovery and scoping before a price can be agreed.

## What about booking, membership or web-app features?

Some websites sit between these categories. A brochure or lead-generation site might also need appointment booking, gated resources, calculators, client portals or third-party integrations.

Those features do not automatically require a completely different website type, but they do affect:

- Build cost and delivery time
- Accessibility and testing
- Data protection and security
- Ongoing subscriptions or transaction fees
- Who is responsible when an integration changes

Treat each feature as a business decision. If a simple enquiry form and a short follow-up call achieve the same outcome, custom functionality may add cost without adding value.


## A practical checklist for choosing

Work through these questions before asking for a quote:

1. **Where do good customers currently come from?** Referrals may point towards a focused brochure site; search-led growth usually needs a broader lead-generation approach.
2. **What should a visitor be able to do?** Check credibility, make an enquiry, book, pay or manage something?
3. **How much content can you support?** A twenty-page plan is not useful if nobody can supply or maintain the information.
4. **How often will the site change?** Regular changes favour an ongoing working relationship; a stable offer may suit a one-off build.
5. **Who will own the technical responsibility?** Hosting, backups, monitoring and future changes still need an owner after launch.
6. **What would make the project commercially worthwhile?** Define a result you can recognise, even if it is not a perfect numerical target.


## Common mistakes to avoid

The problems I see most often are not caused by a particular platform. They start earlier:

- Choosing features before defining the customer journey
- Copying a competitor's structure without knowing whether it works
- Building pages around internal departments rather than customer questions
- Treating mobile as a smaller version of desktop
- Assuming more pages automatically create more search visibility
- Commissioning a site without agreeing who supplies content and approvals

A website is a working business tool. Design matters, but clarity about its job matters first.


## The best next step

If you mainly need credibility, keep the scope focused and execute it well. If the website needs to create demand, allow for research, stronger service content and continued improvement. If customers genuinely need to transact online, plan the operational system around the shop as carefully as the shop itself.
